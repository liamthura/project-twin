This is the page that surprises people, so it comes with the blunt version
first.

<Callout type="warn">
**Nothing reads your messages in the background.**

MCP tools only run when a client calls them. MyGist does not observe your
conversation. It cannot notice that you said something worth remembering unless
the client explicitly asks it to look.
</Callout>

So, by default:

- The assistant **reads** your persona when you ask about yourself.
- The assistant **writes** to it when you explicitly ask it to.
- Anything it merely *infers* goes to a queue you review — never straight into
  your persona.

That is deliberate. A tool that silently recorded everything you typed would be
a different product, and a harder one to trust with this kind of data.

## Two ways things get in

| You say | Tool | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| "Add Datadog to my skills" | `persona_modify` | Written immediately |
| *(mentions in passing that they now run the on-call dashboards)* | `propose_update` | Lands in your review queue |

The rule is one line: **explicit instruction writes, inference proposes.** No
confidence score decides on your behalf, and `propose_update` cannot write to
your persona at all.

## Turning proposals on

If you want a conversation where things get noticed, say so at the start:

> For this conversation, call `propose_update` when you notice something
> durable about me.

Worth putting in your client's system prompt or project instructions if you
want it always on. That is the closest thing to automatic capture that exists —
and it is still the client doing the work, not the server.

## What a proposal carries

Every proposal must include two things, or the server rejects it:

- **`rationale`** — why the agent thinks this is durable, in its own words.
  This is what you read when deciding.
- **`evidence`** — your own words that prompted it. An agent that cannot quote
  you has inferred too far, and the requirement is what stops the queue filling
  with plausible guesses.

Each row also names the tool that proposed it — "Claude Desktop", "Cursor",
"Codex" — so you can tell which of your clients is over-eager.

## The two surfaces

Proposals come in two kinds, and they get their own tabs because they need
different amounts of thought.

### Inbox — things with a known home

Typed, schema-valid changes: a skill level that moved, a project that finished,
a person worth remembering. **Approve**, **edit then approve**, or **reject**.

Approving writes through exactly the same path `persona_modify` uses, so every
duplicate check and validation applies. Editing first is expected — agents get
details slightly wrong often enough that correcting before approving is normal.

### Observations — things with no home yet

Durable but ambiguous, where nothing in the schema fits. "Wants the
recommendation first, then the reasoning" is real, and there is no field for it.

Two actions: **promote** it into a typed entry wherever you think it belongs, or
**delete** it. Observations are a staging area, not storage — they feed no
scope, so no agent reads them until you have promoted them into real data.

If the same kind of observation keeps appearing and nothing fits, that is a
signal to add a section for it. See
[section packs](/run/section-packs).

## Two things that stop the queue rotting

**Rejected claims never come back.** Reject a proposal and it becomes a
tombstone: no agent, in any client, can raise the same thing again. Without
this, every tool re-suggests what you already declined, forever.

**The same claim from three tools is one row.** Repeat proposals increment a
counter instead of stacking up, so you see "seen 3×" — which is useful signal
rather than clutter.

Observations are also bounded: the newest 50 are kept, and rows you have never
looked at are protected from being trimmed.

## The honest limitation

Capture depends on the client actually calling the tool, and clients vary:

- Some do it readily when asked.
- Some forget after a handful of turns.
- None do it unprompted.

If accuracy matters more than convenience, write the entry yourself — see
[Writing](/use/writing) — or use [the editor](/use/editor). Treat proposals as
a way to catch things you would otherwise lose, not as a reliable record.

## What to do at the end of a good conversation

The practical middle ground: when a conversation has produced something worth
keeping, ask for it directly.

> Anything from this conversation worth proposing to my persona?

The client reviews the thread, sends proposals, and you clear them in one pass
from the Review tab.
