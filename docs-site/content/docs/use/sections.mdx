title: What goes in each section
description: A tour of all ten sections, what belongs in them, and what to say to get something stored.
---

The most common question about MyGist is "what am I supposed to put in it?"
This page answers that section by section.

For each one you get: what it holds, what to say out loud to add something, and
what *not* to put there. Field names are named where they help, but the full,
authoritative list always comes from asking `get_schema` — see
[Concepts](/use/concepts#entities) for why these docs do not copy it.

<Callout>
**A rule of thumb for all ten.** MyGist is a context provider, not a task
manager or a diary. Store things that would still be true and still be useful
next month. Every entry costs tokens in every conversation that reads it.
</Callout>

## Profile

**Who you are.** Name, preferred name, current role, organisation, location,
nationality, languages, a short bio, contact links, plus your work history and
education.

Work experience and education are lists, and each entry can carry its own
highlights — the two or three things worth remembering about that job or that
course. A job entry can also carry the skills it involved. Education entries can
hold coursework and clubs.

> I'm a backend developer at Acme, based in Manchester.
> Add my Northumbria BSc in Computer Science, 2022 to 2025.
> Add "Led the migration to Postgres" as a highlight at Acme.
> My GitHub is github.com/liamthura.

**Not here:** career ambitions. Those are goals now — see below.

## Goals

**What you are working toward.** Each goal has a title, and optionally a type
(career, learning, personal, and so on), a status, a target date, why it matters
to you, and free notes.

The `why` field earns its keep. A goal with a reason attached lets an assistant
tell you when a suggestion actually conflicts with what you want.

> I want to move into a platform engineering role within two years.
> My goal is to finish the Rust book by December.
> Mark the "learn Rust" goal as in progress.

**Not here:** this week's to-do list. Goals should outlive a sprint.

## Knowledge

**What you know, and what you are keeping an eye on.** Two separate lists.

**Domains** are subjects you have a relationship with — Postgres, distributed
systems, French cooking. Each has a level, so "I know a bit of Rust" and "I have
worked in Python for eight years" are stored differently. Domains can carry
reference links.

**Mental tabs** are topics you are currently tracking but have not resolved:
an approach you are evaluating, a debate you are following, a decision you are
sitting with. They have a status, so a tab can be closed once it is settled.

> Add Postgres to my skills — I'm confident with it.
> I'm learning Rust at the moment.
> I'm keeping an eye on whether to move off Alembic. Add that as a mental tab.

**Not here:** things you learned. Those go in the learning log.

## Preferences

**How you want to be worked with.** Four things live here:

| | Holds |
|---|---|
| **Code style** | Language conventions, formatting, patterns you want or avoid |
| **Communication** | Tone, detail level, locale, and the shape of a good answer |
| **Learning style** | How you take in new material |
| **Likes and dislikes** | Small taste facts, each with a stance |

You can also store **mood overrides** — a named mood, with the tone and detail
level that suits it. "When I'm debugging, be terse."

> I prefer short answers with the code first and the explanation after.
> Use British spelling.
> I don't like meetings before 10am.
> When I'm tired, keep it to bullet points.

<Callout>
Preferences are special: this section rides along with **every** read scope, not
just some. However narrow a client's request, it still finds out how you like
answers written. That is deliberate — it is the one thing that should never be
scoped away.
</Callout>

## Projects

**What you are building.** Two lists.

**Projects** have a name, a description, a status, tags, notes, highlights, and
reference links. Both work projects and side projects belong here.

**Top of mind** is the short list of what is occupying you right now. It is
meant to be small and churny — a handful of items, replaced often. Entries that
have not been touched in 30 days get flagged as possibly stale when read.

> I'm building Solterra, a solar output tracker in Python.
> Set Solterra's status to active.
> Top of mind: getting the deploy pipeline green.

**Not here:** individual tasks. "Fix the login bug" is a ticket, not context.

## Lifestyle

**Life outside work.** Five lists:

- **Hobbies** — with a skill level, a status (still doing it or not), notes, and
  "specifics" for the details that matter, like which instrument or which route.
- **Interests** — lighter than a hobby. Things you are into without practising.
- **Personality traits** — how you would describe yourself.
- **Values** — what you care about.
- **Wellness** — sleep patterns per day type, when your energy peaks, what
  reliably stresses you out.

> I play badminton, intermediate level, about twice a week.
> I usually sleep around 1am on weekdays and wake at 8.
> My energy peaks late at night.
> Tight deadlines with unclear scope stress me out.

## Circle

**People in your life.** Each connection has a name, the relationship, traits
you associate with them, and notes.

This is context, not a contact book. Store what an assistant would need in order
to help you write to that person or think about that relationship — not their
phone number.

> My manager is Dana. She's direct and prefers written updates over meetings.
> Add my brother Sam — we talk about running.

## Learning log

**What you learned, decided, or realised.** The most conversational of the
sections, and the one capture fills most often.

Each entry has a topic and details. It can also carry the source, tags, key
decisions made, follow-up items, and metadata about the conversation it came out
of. Decisions and follow-ups are their own editable lists, not free text.

Crucially, this is not just for technical facts. "I realised that job titles
don't guarantee commitment" is exactly the kind of entry it is for.

> Log this: I worked out why our search was missing paraphrases — no embeddings
> were configured.
> Add a follow-up to that entry: check the backfill after deploying.

**Not here:** things you know. Those are knowledge domains. The log is about
the moment of learning, with its context attached.

## Media *(optional, off by default)*

**Taste, not a watchlist.** Films, books, music, games — with a kind, a status,
your reaction, a link and notes.

The point is to let an assistant recommend well, or catch a reference. It is not
meant to track what you consumed and when.

> I loved Arrival. Add it to my media.
> I'm halfway through Project Hail Mary and enjoying it.

Turn it on in the Sections manager first — see [the editor](/use/editor#sections).

## Aesthetics *(optional, off by default)*

**Visual taste.** Named styles with a domain, a stance (you like it or you do
not), whether it is a primary influence, notes, and references.

Useful if you ask assistants for design or image work often, and pointless
otherwise. That is why it is opt-in.

> I like brutalist web design. I really don't like glassmorphism.

## Turning sections on and off

Media and aesthetics start off. Profile, preferences and the learning log can
never be turned off. Everything else is on by default and can be disabled.

Disabling a section does not delete anything. The data stays exactly where it
is; the section simply stops being offered to clients and stops appearing in the
editor, and comes back unchanged when you re-enable it.
